# Board to Life

A teacher photographs a drawing on the whiteboard, such as a ramp, a ball and a row of dominoes. The app reads the drawing
into a structured scene, and the teacher checks that reading against the photo and approves it. The projector then
plays the scene with real physics. Playback pauses at a predict point ("Will the last domino fall?") so the class can
commit to an answer before the reveal.

This is a visual tool that the teacher authors and approves. It is not a chatbot: nothing reaches the class until the
teacher has approved it, and the physics engine computes the outcome rather than a script choreographing it.

## Built during the event

Everything in this repository was written from scratch for the Meta Global AI Developer Hackathon (Indie track). Work
started on Oct 26, 2026, the event's kickoff day. No code was carried over from earlier projects. The test drawing in
`public/samples/` was generated by `scripts/draw-sample.mjs` in this repository.

## Scene readers

`/api/understand` and `/api/draft-questions` use the **stub** unless `.env` or `.env.local` says otherwise. The stub
always returns the same hand-written scene for the test drawing (`src/scene/fixtures.ts`), and fixed questions for it,
without looking at the photo. The check screen says which reader produced the scene.

The real reader (`server/sceneReader.ts`) sends the photo to Muse Spark with a prompt listing the body vocabulary and a
JSON schema the reply must follow. Two providers serve it through the same OpenAI-compatible interface:

| Reader | Model | Key in `.env.local` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `meta` | `muse-spark-1.3` on Meta Model API, Standard mode | `MODEL_API_KEY` |
| `baseten` | `meta-models/muse-spark-open` on Baseten | `BASETEN_API_KEY` |

Copy `.env.example` to `.env` or `.env.local` (both ignored by git) and fill in the keys. Set `SCENE_READER=meta` or
`SCENE_READER=baseten` there to make the dev server call that model; each photo then costs a model call. The model
reports positions on a 0-1000 grid. The reader turns them into a scene 1200 units on its long edge, so the physics plays
the same whatever the camera's resolution. Anything outside the vocabulary must come back as an unknown mark. The
reply goes through the same validation as the stub's, and a reply that fails validation is shown as an error.

What the reader does with the reply, each step added because a measured run needed it:

- **Board legend.** The prompt teaches a legend: a hatched rectangle is a fixed wall, a plain one can move, a circle
  with a dot is a pulley, a triangle is a pivot, a straight line between two things is a rope, a zigzag between two
  things is a spring, a rectangle on two small circles is a cart, and "5 kg" next to something is its mass. Without it, both models read every wall as a domino. The test
  drawings follow the legend.
- **Marker line width.** The model measures shapes to the outer edge of the marker line and reports how thick the
  line is. The reader takes that off: dominoes measured with the line were wide enough to stop the chain toppling.
- **Floors as lines.** A floor given without a height is treated as the drawn line on top of the ground.
- **Bounded output.** Ids must be short and plain, and every text field has a length limit and no unlisted fields.
  Baseten otherwise returned ids thousands of characters long.
- **Faded strokes ignored.** The prompt says to ignore faint marks left from an erased drawing.
- **Bodies at both ends.** Validation now rejects ropes, joints and contact triggers that point at a rope. A Baseten
  scene did this, and it would have crashed the physics.

Measured on the 8 check drawings (Oct 27; `npm run drawings -- --reader <name>`), last run of each:

| Reader | Read correctly | Typical call | Slowest call | Cost per run |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `meta`, default effort | 7 of 8 (and in the run before) | 19 s | 28 s | $0.20 |
| `meta`, `--effort low` | 4 of 8 | 7 s | 9 s | $0.08 |
| `baseten` | 3 of 8 | 23 s | 84 s | $0.28 |

Meta's one miss is the same in both runs: one domino after the gap placed just outside the matching tolerance.
Low effort is faster but unreliable: in one read it squeezed the vertical coordinates by about 15%, and in another it
produced a garbled field name. Baseten is mostly right about what is in the drawing, but it often misplaces the ramp
and the ball. These are clean, generated drawings; real whiteboard photos have not been measured yet. `npm run photos` reads your
own photos from `check-photos/` (ignored by git; its `SHOT-LIST.md` says what to draw) and scores each on the count of
each kind, what happens to items picked by position, and whether doodles are flagged. Next to each read it saves the
photo beside the scene as read.

Until real photos are taken, `npm run photos:mock` makes stand-ins for the shot list: each check scene sketched with a
new hand, on a framed whiteboard filling about 60% of the shot, at 4032 × 3024, tilted slightly or (for `01-chain-angle`)
about 30 degrees off to the side, in uneven light. They test framing, angle and scale; they are still generated drawings,
not real handwriting. Meta on the 10 mocks (Oct 28): **10 of 10** correct, including both stays-up outcomes and the
flagged doodle. Typical read 18 s; the pendulum took 71 s, longer than the server's 60 s limit at the time, so the app would have failed it. $0.27 for the run.

After adding springs, ropes over pulleys, carts and written masses, the check set has 16 drawings: 4 new scenes, one
for each, plus a photo-style version of every scene. Each new scene has a negative control in its tests: the same swing
on a rope misses, the same push on a block stops short, and the seesaw's domino stays up with the masses swapped or
left off. Meta on all 16 (Oct 28, default effort): **14 of 16** read correctly, and the outcome was right for all 16.
The two misses were the clean spring and seesaw drawings, each with one item placed just past the matching tolerance.
Typical call 21 s, slowest 40 s, $0.37 per run. The run before, with square dominoes and a single domino under the
pulley swing, scored 9 of 16.

Muse Spark Open on Baseten on the same 16 (Oct 29): **5 of 16** read correctly. Of the other 11, nine named every item
right and gave the right outcome but placed a ramp, ball, pulley or block past the matching tolerance, and two replies
failed validation with a duplicate id (`block-1`, `predict-1`). Every outcome it could be scored on was right. Typical
call 22 s, slowest 36 s, $0.49 per run. The live site uses Meta's Muse Spark.

Every model call, reading or drafting, has a budget of 120 s and tries once more after a dropped connection, a busy or
failing server (429 or 5xx) or a reply that is not JSON. A rejected key or request is not retried, and neither is a call
that used up the budget. The browser waits 130 s, longer than the server, so the server's own error reaches the teacher.

Each paid run saves every reply, its token usage and its cost under `check-runs/` (ignored by git) and adds the cost
to `check-runs/ledger.jsonl`.

### Question drafting

With `SCENE_READER` set to a model, `/api/draft-questions` asks the same model for the predict questions
(`server/questionDrafter.ts`). The physics decides the answer, and the model only writes the question:

- Before asking, the server runs the scene to rest and gives the model what happened, in order ("0.6 s: ball touches
  domino-1", "2.2 s: domino-5 falls over"), plus which items end up fallen. The explanation is told from those events
  rather than guessed. Without the event list, one draft had the dominoes falling from the wrong end.
- The reply's schema only allows ids that are in the scene: real predict points, items that can tip over, and real
  items to cite. The prompt lists the words for body kinds the drawing does not have, which grounding rejects.
- Every question is yes or no on whether one item falls over, and is checked for grounding before it reaches the
  teacher, who approves it as before.

Measured with `npm run questions` (the 4 expected check scenes) and on the 8 scenes Meta read in its last check-set
run (Oct 28): all 12 drafts grounded, and every explanation agreed with the physics. Each draft takes 5 to 18 s and
costs about a cent. One explanation on a photo read was clumsy, naming dominoes in the wrong order of position. In the
app with the Meta reader, the sample took about 16 s to read and 6 s to draft.

## Run it

Requires Node 22 or later (developed on Node 26).

```bash
npm install
npm run dev
```

Open http://127.0.0.1:5173.

1. **Capture**: upload a photo, use the webcam, or click "Use the sample drawing". The photo is turned upright using
   its EXIF orientation and scaled so its long edge is at most 1600 px, then sent as JPEG.
2. **Check**: the check screen opens as soon as the scene is read; the predict questions are drafted while the teacher
   looks it over, and Approve stays disabled until they arrive. While the scene is read, the screen shows the seconds
   elapsed against the usual 20 s, and Cancel goes back to capture; the browser gives up after 130 s. The scene is drawn over the photo. Items below 70% confidence are dashed in amber, and marks the reader
   did not understand are boxed in red and will not be animated. The drafted question is labelled *Draft · not shown to
   the class*.
3. **Approve**: this stores a separate, labelled approved copy. "Present to the class" stays disabled until then.
4. **Present**: full screen. Press Play (or Space). Playback pauses at the predict point; Reveal resumes it. The result
   is computed from the simulation ("Yes: domino-5 fell over"). Replay (or R) restarts from the same approved scene.
   The canvas redraws only while something moves, and positions are blended between physics steps so motion stays
   smooth on displays faster than 60 Hz.

   The scene plays **on the photo itself**. Each moving item's own strokes are painted over in the board colour where it
   was drawn, and the item is drawn again wherever the physics puts it, so the class sees their own board come to life.
   The patch colour is the median of a ring just around the item (`src/present/onPhoto.ts`), which follows uneven light
   and ignores other strokes crossing the ring. Fixed items (floor, walls, ramps, pulleys) stay as the teacher drew them;
   where a patch overlaps one, the photo is put back inside that item's outline. "Show the clean drawing" switches to
   plain strokes on a blank board, with written labels such as "5 kg" riding along with their item.

## Live site

The app is deployed on Vercel at https://board-to-life.vercel.app. `npm run build:vercel` builds it in Vercel's Build
Output format (`scripts/build-vercel.mjs`): the app as static files, and `/api/understand` and `/api/draft-questions`
as Node functions bundled from `server/vercel.ts`, which runs the same middleware as the dev server.

Because each photo is a paid model call, the API answers only requests that carry the access code when `ACCESS_CODE`
is set in the project's environment. The live link has the code in its address (`?code=…`); the app keeps it and sends
it as the `x-access-code` header. Without it the API answers 401 and the app says to open the link from the submission
page. The model keys and `SCENE_READER` are set in the Vercel project's environment, never in the repository.

### Stub modes (error states)

The "Stub mode" menu in the header makes the stubs misbehave so the error screens can be checked:

| Mode | What happens | What the teacher sees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ok` | fixed scene and questions | check screen |
| `error` | `/understand` returns 503 | message and Try again |
| `timeout` | `/understand` waits 145 s; the client gives up at 130 s | "did not answer within 130 seconds" and Try again |
| `invalid scene` | the scene contains a `spring`, which is outside the vocabulary | validation errors and Try again |
| `ungrounded draft` | the draft cites `domino-9`, which is not in the scene | the check screen with a grounding error in place of the questions, and Draft again |

## Quality gates

```bash
npm run check
```

This runs ESLint (typescript-eslint strict), `tsc --noEmit` (strict, `noUncheckedIndexedAccess`,
`exactOptionalPropertyTypes`) and Vitest.

| Spec | Covers |
| --- | --- |
| `src/scene/schema.spec.ts` | scene schema v1: the full body vocabulary, out-of-vocabulary kinds rejected, confidence range, bounds, duplicate ids, dangling references, unknown marks kept |
| `src/scene/questions.spec.ts` | grounding: a draft citing a missing item, observing a missing item, using a missing predict point, or naming a body kind the scene lacks is rejected |
| `src/physics/simulation.spec.ts` | determinism (two runs give identical states), exact pause step, the test drawing knocks over all five dominoes, a negative control (remove `domino-3` and the last domino stays standing), the same outcome with 1, 2 or 4 substeps, and a fast ball that does not pass through a thin wall |
| `src/present/playback.spec.ts` | play, pause exactly at the predict point, reveal, computed outcome, replay to the same outcome |
| `src/session/store.spec.ts` | nothing is presentable before approval; no approval while questions are still drafting; questions drafted for a replaced scene are ignored; draft and approved copies are separate |
| `src/capture/downscale.spec.ts` | photo sizing: long edge capped at 1600 px, aspect kept, small photos untouched |
| `src/present/interpolate.spec.ts` | blending body states between physics steps |
| `src/present/redraw.spec.ts` | drawing every frame while moving, one final frame once still, and again after a resize |
| `server/stub.spec.ts` | stub endpoints, including rejection of invalid scenes and ungrounded drafts |
| `src/checks/*.spec.ts` | drawing check set: pinned outcomes and their controls, drawings match their scenes, photo versions keep the same expectations and carry erased strokes, the HTTP reader (request shape, server errors, missing scene), item matching (renamed, nudged, misread, rescaled, reversed ramps, ropes, unflagged and invented marks), replay with a perfect reader, the stub, a changed outcome, an invalid scene and a failing reader |
| `src/api.spec.ts` | client timeout, server error, network failure, and non-JSON error bodies become clear, retryable messages |

## Drawing check set

`src/checks/` holds four drawings with the scene each one should be read as, and what should happen when it plays:

| Drawing | Expected outcome |
| --- | --- |
| `ramp-ball-dominoes` (the sample) | the last domino falls |
| `dominoes-wide-gap` | the third domino falls; the last one stays up because the gap is too wide |
| `pendulum-knock` (pulley, rope, ball) | the swinging ball knocks all three dominoes over |
| `wall-in-the-way` (with an unrecognised zigzag) | a short wall stops the ball, so the domino stays up |

Each drawing also has a **photo-style** version (`*-photo`, a 1600 × 1067 JPEG, the size the app sends after
downscaling). It adds uneven classroom light, a glare patch that washes out part of the drawing, a warm cast, slight
blur, sensor noise and phone-grade compression. The three sketched photos also carry faint, offset strokes of a
different, half-erased drawing under them. A reader that turns those strokes into bodies fails on extra items.

The three new drawings are sketched from their expected scenes by `src/checks/sketch.ts`, so a drawing and its
expected scene cannot disagree. A spec fails if a committed drawing no longer matches its scene. The pinned outcomes
are what the physics computes for each expected scene. Two controls show they are not accidents: without the wall the
domino falls, and without the rope the dominoes stay up. `npm run drawings:draw` redraws everything (it needs
`rsvg-convert` and ImageMagick 7), and gives byte-identical files on a rerun.

```bash
npm run drawings                                                   # the stub reader, in-process
npm run drawings -- --reader meta                                  # Muse Spark on Meta Model API (paid)
npm run drawings -- --reader baseten                               # Muse Spark Open on Baseten (paid)
npm run questions                                                  # draft questions for the check scenes with Meta (paid)
npm run photos                                                     # read real photos from check-photos/ with Meta (paid)
npm run photos:mock                                                # make stand-in phone photos in check-photos/mock/
npm run drawings -- --reader http --url http://127.0.0.1:5173/api/understand   # a running server's endpoint
```

This sends each drawing to a scene reader, then reports, for each drawing:

- **Items:** each item is matched by kind and position, scaled to the drawing size. The report shows the worst
  position error and lists missing and extra items.
- **Unknown marks:** marks the reader did not flag, and bodies it invented inside an unknown mark.
- **Outcome:** whether the scene as read gives the expected outcome when played.

It exits with 1 unless every drawing passes. The `http` reader posts each image to a running `/api/understand`. That
checks the whole endpoint, including upload handling and validation, so the real scene reader needs no change to the
check set once it sits behind that endpoint. Today both readers reach the stub. It always returns the sample scene, so
it reads 2 of the 8 drawings correctly: the sample and its photo. That shows the check can tell a wrong reading from a
right one.

What the check set still does not cover:

- **Photos taken at an angle.** A real phone photo has perspective. Covering it needs a decision about which frame the
  reader reports coordinates in, and the physics would also need the scene straightened first.
- **Real handwriting.** The sketches are generated, and the only hand-drawn input is the clean sample. A pass here
  does not prove a reader copes with real boards.

## Scene schema v1

Defined in `src/scene/schema.ts`. All positions are in the drawing's own coordinates.

- **items**: `ball`, `block`, `domino`, `ramp`, `wall`, `floor`, `lever`, `pivot`, `pulley`, `cart`, `rope` and
  `spring`. Each item has an `id` and a `confidence` from 0 to 1. Balls, blocks and carts may carry an initial
  `velocity` in drawing units per second, taken from arrows in the drawing. Movable items may carry a `mass` in
  kilograms, read from a written label such as "5 kg"; otherwise their mass comes from their size.
- **links**: a `rope` or `spring` joins two bodies and has the length it was drawn at. A rope can run `over` a pulley.
- **joints**: `pin` joints between two items at a point.
- **labels**: handwritten text, optionally attached to an item.
- **unknownMarks**: anything outside the vocabulary. These marks are flagged with a note and are never turned into
  bodies.
- **predictPoints**: a `time` trigger (seconds) or a `contact` trigger (two items touch).

Predict questions (`src/scene/questions.ts`) cite scene item ids. Each one observes an item (for example "fallen")
whose outcome the simulation decides.

## Physics settings

Matter.js steps 60 times per second, with 4 substeps per step, so a fast ball cannot pass through a thin domino
between steps. Gravity is scaled up from Matter's default (drawing units are treated as roughly millimetres). At
Matter's default gravity the domino chain crept along at its friction limit and its outcome changed with the step size.
The ball is light, like a hollow ball, and dominoes have a friction of 0.6. With these settings, the test drawing gives
the same outcome with 1, 2 or 4 substeps, and so does the version with a gap in the chain. The predict point is the
moment the ball first touches the first domino.

How each kind behaves:

| Kind | Physics |
| --- | --- |
| rope | a rigid link the length it was drawn; over a pulley, the two sides together never get longer and it only pulls, so the heavier side goes down |
| spring | a soft link (stiffness 0.004) at rest at its drawn length: a ball on it stretches it to about 1.7 times and bounces |
| cart | a body with a wheel pinned at each bottom corner; the wheels have no friction. Matter.js loses a lot of energy on rolling contact under our strong gravity (a pushed ball stops within about 200 units), so ideal, gliding wheels stand in for real rolling |
| mass | "1 kg" weighs about as much as a domino drawn at the usual size |
| domino | rounded edges (3 units). With square ones, a domino read just a few units too wide was shoved along the floor by a flat push instead of tipping |

## Known limitations

- Webcam capture could not be tested with a real camera here. The capture-to-check path was exercised with a
  synthetic canvas stream in place of the camera.
- Determinism is tested within one JavaScript engine. Different browsers may compute floating-point maths slightly
  differently, so the same scene is not guaranteed to give bit-identical motion on two different browsers.
- A rope over a pulley is drawn as two straight lines through the pulley's centre, not around its rim.
- The cart's wheels glide rather than roll, and the new kinds' physics has not been checked against real carts,
  springs or pulleys.
- The physics rendering is plain canvas strokes, not yet the hand-inked style.
- Playing on the photo assumes the photo is the drawing, square-on: a strongly angled photo puts patches and redrawn
  items slightly off the teacher's strokes. Where a moving item overlaps a fixed one, such as a ball resting on a
  ramp, a sliver of its original stroke stays on the photo. Labels attached to a moving item stay where they were
  written on the photo; only the clean drawing moves them.
- Frame rate and the smoothness gained from blending between steps have not been measured on a real display: the
  development browser pane throttles animation frames whenever it is hidden.
- The ball shoves domino-1 about 60–130 drawing units along the floor before the chain topples. That is what the engine
  computes for this drawing, but it has not been checked against a real ball and real dominoes.
